Most land problems in Kenya are not about who owns a parcel. They are about where it is. The title names a parcel; the survey records say where its corners are; the beacons on the ground are supposed to sit on those corners. When any of the three disagree, someone loses land, a wall comes down or a sale stalls.

A boundary check puts the three side by side. We start from the records you have, which may be a deed plan with coordinates or a registry map that shows the parcel only approximately. On site we search for each beacon, measure it with RTK GNSS or a total station and photograph it. Back in the office we compare the measured positions with the record and with the fences and buildings around them.

The report says plainly what was found: which beacons are in place and agree with the record, which are missing or disturbed, and whether anything built sits across the line. If the next step is a licensed surveyor, a lawyer or the Land Registrar, you go with measured facts rather than a description.

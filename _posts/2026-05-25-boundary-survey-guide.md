An official search tells you who holds the title to a parcel. It does not tell you where the parcel is. Most land problems we are asked to look at come from that gap: the buyer paid for the parcel on the map, and later found that the beacons on the ground, the neighbour's fence or the road told a different story.

Checking the beacons before you pay closes that gap. It is quicker and far cheaper than any dispute that follows.

## What a beacon is

A beacon is a physical mark at a corner of a parcel: usually a concrete post or an iron pin set in concrete, placed by a surveyor when the parcel was created. Each one corresponds to a point in the survey records. When the beacons are in place and agree with the records, the boundary between them is known.

Beacons get lost. They are buried by road works and farming, knocked over by vehicles and, sometimes, moved on purpose. A parcel that was surveyed decades ago may have none left that can be seen without digging.

## Fixed and general boundaries

Kenyan land law distinguishes between two kinds of boundary, and which one applies to your parcel changes what a check can confirm.

**Fixed boundaries** are defined by survey: the corners have measured coordinates or dimensions in the survey records, and the plan that comes with the title (often a deed plan) shows them. A surveyor can recompute where each corner should be and compare it with the beacon in the ground.

**General boundaries** are shown on a registry index map, usually drawn from aerial photographs during land adjudication. Under the Land Registration Act, 2012, such a map shows the boundary only approximately; on the ground the boundary is the hedge, ditch or fence the map was drawn from. A check here compares the features on the ground with the map, rather than measuring to the centimetre.

Much rural freehold land has general boundaries. Town plots and leasehold land usually have fixed ones. The records you collect tell you which applies.

## Records to collect before the check

Ask the seller for copies of:

- The title or certificate of lease, and an official search dated within the last few days. Searches are now done online through Ardhisasa where the registry has moved to it, or at the land registry.
- The plan the parcel was registered with: a deed plan, a survey plan reference, or an extract of the registry index map showing the parcel and its neighbours.
- For a parcel recently cut from a larger one, the subdivision documents, including the approved survey of the new parcels.

A surveyor will use these to decide what to measure against. If the seller cannot produce any plan at all, treat that as a warning sign in itself.

## What happens on site

A boundary check usually takes a day in the field.

1. **Search.** We look for each beacon where the records say it should be, clearing vegetation and, where needed, probing for buried marks.
2. **Measure.** Each beacon found is measured with RTK GNSS or a total station, in the coordinate system the records use.
3. **Compare.** The measured positions are compared with the record: are the beacons where they should be, and are the distances between them right?
4. **Look around.** Fences, walls, buildings, paths and power lines near the boundary are measured too, along with the access road and any river or wetland at the edge of the land.
5. **Photograph.** Every beacon and every problem is photographed and located, so the report can be checked by someone who was not there.

## What the results can mean

**All beacons found and in agreement with the record.** The parcel on the ground is the parcel on paper. This is the result you want before paying.

**Some beacons missing.** Common, and not necessarily a problem if enough survive to fix the boundary. Replacing a beacon on a registered boundary must be done by a licensed land surveyor; the cost and timing are worth agreeing with the seller before the sale.

**Beacons present but in the wrong place.** A beacon several metres from where the record puts it has been disturbed or moved. Do not proceed until the reason is clear.

**Something built across the line.** A neighbour's wall, a house or a road inside the parcel will not go away by itself. Get advice from an advocate before paying.

**Area smaller than stated.** Small differences come from rounding and old measurement methods. Large ones mean the parcel on the ground is not the parcel being sold.

## Warning signs

- The seller cannot produce any survey plan or map extract.
- The beacons have been "replaced recently" by someone other than a licensed surveyor.
- The fence line does not follow the beacons.
- The access shown on the map is not the access used on the ground.
- Part of the plot is a river bank, a wetland or a road verge.
- Neighbours tell a different story about where the boundary is.

None of these means the sale is impossible. Each means you should find out more before you pay.

## When something is wrong

A boundary check is evidence, not a ruling. If it shows a problem, the next step depends on the problem. A missing beacon goes to a licensed land surveyor. Disagreements with a neighbour over the boundary of registered land go first to the Land Registrar, who has the power to determine them before a court will hear the matter. Ownership questions go to an advocate. In every case, going with measured coordinates and photographs makes the conversation shorter.

## A checklist before you pay

- [ ] Title or certificate of lease seen, and a current official search
- [ ] The survey plan, deed plan or registry map extract for the parcel
- [ ] Beacons located and measured, with a written report
- [ ] Access confirmed on the ground
- [ ] No buildings, roads or reserves inside the boundary
- [ ] Any problem resolved, or priced into the deal, before payment
